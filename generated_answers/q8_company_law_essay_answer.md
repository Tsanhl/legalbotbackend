Part I: Introduction

English law is coherent only if company personality, veil piercing, and parent-company liability are kept as distinct doctrines rather than treated as one general fairness inquiry. Separate corporate personality remains the orthodox baseline, true veil piercing survives only as a narrow anti-evasion doctrine, and parent-company liability operates through ordinary negligence principles rather than through disregard of the subsidiary's separate existence (Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22; Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34; Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe [2019] UKSC 20). The modern law is therefore strongest on taxonomy and weaker on the broader question of group accountability.

That distinction matters because the question asks about coherence, not simply about whether claimants usually win. A system can be coherent yet normatively unsatisfying, or normatively attractive in some cases yet conceptually muddled. English law's current position is strongest when it insists on doctrinal separation. It is weakest when that separation leaves obvious accountability gaps to be filled only indirectly and expensively through tort litigation.

Part II: Separate Corporate Personality and the Baseline Rule

Any evaluation has to begin with the proposition that incorporation creates a distinct legal person. That remains the foundation for limited liability, entity ownership of assets, and the stability of ordinary commercial dealing (Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22). The continuing force of that principle matters because much criticism of corporate groups assumes that separateness is merely a technical obstacle to justice. In fact it also performs clear rule-of-law work. Creditors know which entity they contracted with, shareholders know the ordinary limit of their exposure, and courts avoid converting hard cases into open-ended judicial discretion (Macaura v Northern Assurance Co Ltd [1925] AC 619; Lee v Lee's Air Farming Ltd [1961] AC 12).

The same logic explains why English law has long resisted the claim that a corporate group should be treated as a single legal unit simply because it behaves as one economic enterprise. The refusal to accept a broad single-economic-unit doctrine is often criticised as formalistic, but it preserves ex ante certainty and stops courts collapsing entity boundaries merely because the group structure is unattractive on the facts (Adams v Cape Industries plc [1990] Ch 433). That position can look tough on claimants, especially where the operating subsidiary is thinly capitalised or asset poor. Even so, it keeps the core rule clear: the company form is not ignored simply because a different allocation of loss would appear fairer after the event.

There is also an institutional point here. Company law uses bright-line incorporation rules because investors, lenders, and counterparties organise their affairs on the assumption that entity boundaries have legal significance. If courts routinely blurred those boundaries after the event, much of the value of incorporation would be lost. That is why the continuing significance of separate personality is not merely historical reverence. It is the condition that makes later doctrines such as veil piercing and parent-company negligence intelligible as exceptions or parallel routes rather than as ad hoc exercises of judicial sympathy.

The baseline rule also helps explain why coherence in this area cannot mean perfect claimant protection. The law is trying to balance accountability against a form that facilitates investment, asset partitioning, and ordinary commercial planning. A system that made parent shareholders routinely answerable for subsidiary debts might produce broader compensation, but it would also rewrite the economic logic of incorporation. English law's refusal to do that is not a sign of doctrinal confusion. It is the starting policy choice around which the narrower exceptions have been built.

Part III: Veil Piercing after Prest

The next question is what the law does when the company form is used abusively. Modern law answers that question narrowly. Earlier cases sometimes used loose language about lifting or piercing the veil, and that language encouraged the impression that courts retained a broad justice-based power to ignore separate personality (Gilford Motor Co Ltd v Horne [1933] Ch 935; Jones v Lipman [1962] 1 WLR 832). Prest imposed much sharper discipline. The distinction between concealment and evasion matters because it shows that many supposed veil cases are not true veil-piercing cases at all (Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34).

On that analysis, concealment cases simply require the court to identify the real facts behind a corporate structure without disregarding legal personality. Evasion cases are different because the company is interposed to frustrate an existing obligation, and only then does true veil piercing arise as a narrow residual response (Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34). That is a genuine gain in coherence. It rejects the older habit of using veil-piercing rhetoric as a substitute for identifying the actual doctrinal basis of liability. The court no longer needs to talk as if there were a free-floating equitable discretion to disregard incorporation whenever justice appears to demand it.

The price, however, is that veil piercing now does very little practical work. Many disputes that once sounded like veil cases are better explained through agency, trust analysis, director liability, unjust enrichment, or ordinary statutory interpretation (VTB Capital plc v Nutritek International Corp [2013] UKSC 5; Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34). That narrowing can be defended. If veil piercing is meant to be a last-resort anti-evasion doctrine, it should remain exceptional because company law depends on stable entity boundaries. Yet the same narrowing also exposes a policy weakness. If the doctrine is confined to a tiny class of evasion cases, it cannot do much to answer broader accountability problems created by strategic risk allocation through group structures.

That is why the modern law is coherent in its treatment of veil piercing but not necessarily satisfying. The doctrine is clearer because it is smaller. What has been gained in taxonomy has been lost in remedial reach. One can therefore say that Prest improved doctrinal coherence while simultaneously confirming that veil piercing is not the main contemporary route for dealing with corporate-group harm.

Part IV: Parent-Company Liability and Group Structures

Parent-company liability is the modern pressure point because it responds to real harms inside corporate groups without openly abandoning separate personality. The decisive point is that these cases are not veil-piercing cases. Liability, if established, turns on the parent's own conduct in relation to the relevant risk, not on the proposition that parent and subsidiary have become one legal person (Chandler v Cape plc [2012] EWCA Civ 525; Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe [2019] UKSC 20). That distinction is conceptually important because it preserves the Salomon baseline while still allowing courts to impose responsibility where the parent has assumed responsibility, exercised significant control, or held itself out as taking charge of relevant operations.

The early modern authority showed that a parent could owe a duty of care where it had superior knowledge and an operational relationship to the subsidiary's working practices capable of generating responsibility (Chandler v Cape plc [2012] EWCA Civ 525). The later Supreme Court cases refined that analysis by stressing that there is no special category of parent-company duty. The correct question is whether ordinary negligence principles are satisfied on the facts, including group policies, management systems, supervision, training, monitoring, or public commitments that show the parent took responsibility in substance rather than merely in aspiration (Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe [2019] UKSC 20).

The insistence on ordinary negligence doctrine is one of the strongest signs of coherence in the modern law. It avoids saying that every corporate group is really one enterprise for all legal purposes. It also avoids using veil-piercing rhetoric as a substitute for factual analysis. If the parent intervened sufficiently in the relevant operations, it may owe a direct duty. If it did not, the subsidiary's separate personality remains decisive. That distinction keeps the doctrines conceptually distinct even where they interact in practice.

Later authority also recognised that courts should not treat group-wide policies as legally irrelevant merely because they are framed at a high level, and that claimants may need disclosure and a realistic view of corporate practice before the true degree of parental involvement can be understood (Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc [2021] UKSC 3). That makes the modern law more responsive than a rigid insistence on subsidiary separateness alone. It also explains why parent-company liability now does much of the practical accountability work that older discussions wrongly attributed to veil piercing.

The weakness is that the route remains fact sensitive, expensive, and uneven. Claimants still need to prove a direct duty-creating relationship. The law does not recognise a general group-enterprise principle, and it does not assume responsibility simply from ownership or strategic influence. As a result, English law keeps its doctrines cleanly separated, but it only partially answers the reality that many corporate groups coordinate risk centrally while distributing liability formally. Conceptual clarity is therefore purchased at the cost of patchy practical accountability and substantial procedural complexity.

There is also an access-to-justice dimension. The more the law insists on granular evidence of operational control, assumption of responsibility, or intervention in local systems, the more valuable internal corporate documents become. That makes disclosure practice central to the effectiveness of the doctrine. In principle, the route is available. In practice, it may be costly to prove and strategically difficult to litigate. That does not make the doctrine incoherent, but it does mean that coherence in theory may coexist with uneven accountability in operation.

Part V: Distinct Doctrines, Incomplete Accountability

The real evaluative difficulty lies here. If coherence means that each doctrine has a stable function, the modern law performs well. Separate personality explains the baseline. Veil piercing handles exceptional anti-evasion cases. Parent-company liability addresses situations where the parent itself has created or controlled the risk (Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22; Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34; Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe [2019] UKSC 20). On that account, the law is far more coherent than older judicial language suggested.

If coherence means that doctrine and commercial reality fit together smoothly, the answer is less favourable. Large groups often behave as integrated businesses while allocating liability through separate entities. English law refuses to recognise that economic reality as a general legal principle. Instead it preserves formal separateness and then reconstructs responsibility case by case through narrow doctrines. That may be a deliberate and defensible policy choice, but it leaves the accountability structure fragmented. The parent may be liable, but only if claimants can prove enough operational involvement. Veil piercing may help, but only in the rare anti-evasion case. The subsidiary remains the primary defendant even when the economic logic of the group points elsewhere.

That criticism should not be overstated. A broader group-enterprise doctrine would create its own uncertainty and might undermine legitimate uses of incorporation. English law's reluctance to invent such a doctrine is therefore not necessarily incoherent. It may instead reflect a clear preference for certainty and targeted exceptions over a general fairness override. The law's weakness is better described as selective accountability than conceptual confusion.

There is, in other words, a difference between conceptual neatness and systemic completeness. Modern doctrine tells courts what box to use, but it does not promise that every morally troubling group-structure case will fall within one of those boxes. That is why the present law can be both coherent and contested. It is coherent because the rules are more clearly separated than before. It remains contested because the separation leaves unresolved the broader policy question of whether modern multinational groups should attract a more explicit doctrine of enterprise responsibility.

That unresolved policy question also explains why the debate keeps returning despite the greater doctrinal order achieved by Prest, Vedanta, and Okpabi. The law has clarified what each doctrine is for, but it has not removed the practical pressure created by complex group structures. So long as corporate groups centralise strategy while decentralising liability, arguments for a broader accountability model will continue even if the existing doctrinal map remains internally coherent.

Part VI: Conclusion

English law now takes a coherent approach in the limited but important sense that it keeps three ideas separate. Separate corporate personality remains the default rule, and that default still performs serious commercial and constitutional work (Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22; Adams v Cape Industries plc [1990] Ch 433). True veil piercing survives only as a narrow anti-evasion doctrine after Prest, rather than as a free-standing discretion to ignore incorporation whenever fairness points the other way (Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013] UKSC 34). Parent-company liability, meanwhile, is not an exception to Salomon but an application of ordinary negligence principles to a parent's own conduct within group operations (Vedanta Resources plc v Lungowe [2019] UKSC 20; Okpabi v Royal Dutch Shell plc [2021] UKSC 3).

That taxonomy is much clearer than older veil rhetoric. The harder criticism is not that the doctrines are confused, but that the overall accountability system remains incomplete. Veil piercing is now so narrow that it rarely matters, while parent-company liability depends on fact-heavy negligence reasoning that may be costly to establish and difficult to litigate. The best verdict is therefore mixed. Modern English law is coherent in classification and method, but only partially satisfactory in policy because the need for corporate accountability is addressed indirectly rather than through a more openly structured group-responsibility doctrine.
