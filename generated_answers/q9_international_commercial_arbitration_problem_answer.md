Part I: Introduction

Atlas presently holds the stronger overall position because it already has an award, London is a pro-arbitration seat, and Meridian's objections must fit within tightly controlled challenge routes and procedural filters rather than a general appeal on the merits (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 1, 67, 68, 70). The key is to separate three different questions that Meridian may try to blur together: jurisdiction, fairness, and enforceability (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V, VI). Those are the routes that matter under English supervisory law and Convention enforcement law (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V, VI).

That distinction matters because a London seat gives the English court primary supervisory authority over the award, while enforcement courts abroad exercise only a limited collateral review (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68; New York Convention, arts V(1)(e), VI). A successful challenge at the seat can destabilise the award across multiple jurisdictions, especially through art V(1)(e) of the New York Convention, whereas refusal of enforcement in one state is ordinarily only local and does not erase the award everywhere (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68; New York Convention, arts V(1)(e), VI).

Atlas should therefore think like an award creditor, not like a fresh claimant (Arbitration Act 1996, section 66; New York Convention, arts III, V, VI). Its practical goal is to preserve the award in England, enforce quickly against assets where Meridian is exposed, and resist any attempt by Meridian to recast weak merits complaints as jurisdictional or due-process complaints (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 34, 66, 67, 68; New York Convention, arts III, V, VI).

Meridian, by contrast, should concentrate on the few arguments that can still do real work: a carefully framed jurisdiction challenge on scope, a serious irregularity complaint if the evidential exclusion was truly unfair, and targeted Convention defences abroad rather than abstract public-policy rhetoric (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 34, 67, 68).

Part II: Jurisdiction and the Scope of the Arbitration Agreement

A. Issue

The first issue is whether Atlas's fraud and bribery allegations fell within the arbitration agreement, and whether the tribunal decided matters outside the reference.

B. Rule

Substantive jurisdiction includes whether there is a valid arbitration agreement, whether the tribunal is properly constituted, and what matters have been submitted to arbitration in accordance with that agreement (Arbitration Act 1996, section 30). A party that objects must raise the point promptly before the tribunal, because failure to do so may trigger loss of the objection under sections 31 and 73 of the 1996 Act (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 31, 73).

Although the tribunal may rule on its own jurisdiction in the first instance, that is not the final word (Arbitration Act 1996, section 30). A proper section 67 application still permits curial review of substantive jurisdiction, but current English law must now be stated more carefully than older authorities alone might suggest (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67, as amended by the Arbitration Act 2025; Dallah Real Estate and Tourism Holding Co v Ministry of Religious Affairs, Government of Pakistan [2010] UKSC 46).

Since the Arbitration Act 2025 reforms, where a participating party challenges a jurisdiction ruling the tribunal has already addressed, rules of court may generally prevent a full rehearing, fresh grounds, or new evidence unless the interests of justice require otherwise (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67, as amended by the Arbitration Act 2025). Dallah therefore remains important historically, but Meridian cannot simply assume a complete do-over at the seat (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67, as amended by the Arbitration Act 2025; Dallah Real Estate and Tourism Holding Co v Ministry of Religious Affairs, Government of Pakistan [2010] UKSC 46).

Meridian also has to satisfy section 70's procedural filter (Arbitration Act 1996, section 70). Any available arbitral review, correction, or section 57 route must generally be exhausted first, and the challenge must be brought within the statutory 28-day period (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 57, 70). That matters because English challenge routes are procedurally narrow as well as substantively narrow (Arbitration Act 1996, section 70).

English law also construes arbitration clauses broadly in commercial contracts (Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40). The modern starting point is that rational business parties who agree arbitration usually intend one-stop adjudication for disputes arising out of their relationship, including serious allegations such as fraud or bribery, unless the wording clearly cuts those matters out or the attack is directed specifically at the arbitration agreement itself (Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40; Arbitration Act 1996, section 7).

English law now also uses a statutory default rule on the law governing the arbitration agreement: if that agreement has no express choice of law of its own, the law of the seat governs, and a main-contract choice-of-law clause does not by itself count as an express choice for the arbitration agreement (Arbitration Act 2025; Enka v Chubb [2020] UKSC 38). That point matters more for validity than for the present scope dispute, but it is now part of the current English framework and displaces reliance on Enka alone (Arbitration Act 2025; Enka v Chubb [2020] UKSC 38).

C. Application

Atlas has the better argument on the main scope question (Arbitration Act 1996, section 7). The dispute arises out of termination of the drilling-services contract and withholding of equipment, and the fraud and bribery allegations appear to be part of the same commercial breakdown rather than a wholly detached wrong (Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40; Arbitration Act 1996, section 7). On those facts, Meridian's attempt to label the allegations "non-contractual" is not decisive, because English law looks to the commercial relationship and the objective scope of the clause, not merely to whether a pleading is framed in contract, tort, fraud, or some mixed category (Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40).

Meridian's stronger point is the narrower one: the tribunal may have decided issues "never properly submitted" (Arbitration Act 1996, section 30(1)(c); Dallah Real Estate and Tourism Holding Co v Ministry of Religious Affairs, Government of Pakistan [2010] UKSC 46). That can be a real jurisdiction objection under section 30(1)(c) if the award travels beyond the pleaded disputes, the terms of reference, or the issues the parties actually argued (Arbitration Act 1996, section 30(1)(c); Dallah Real Estate and Tourism Holding Co v Ministry of Religious Affairs, Government of Pakistan [2010] UKSC 46).

If Atlas pleaded wrongful termination and withholding of equipment but the tribunal made free-standing determinations on broader bribery issues that neither party squarely submitted, Meridian would have a materially better point (Arbitration Act 1996, section 30(1)(c); Dallah Real Estate and Tourism Holding Co v Ministry of Religious Affairs, Government of Pakistan [2010] UKSC 46).

Even so, the facts presently favour Atlas (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 31, 73; Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40). The tribunal rejected the jurisdiction objection during the arbitration, which implies Meridian raised the issue in time and preserved it, but preservation is not the same thing as merit (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 31, 73). If the fraud and bribery allegations were advanced as part of Atlas's case concerning termination and consequential relief, the tribunal will likely be treated as acting within a broadly framed commercial submission rather than stepping outside it (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 31, 73; Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40).

The clause allowing the tribunal to award "all remedies available under the governing law" also weakens Meridian's attempt to portray the award of lost profits and declaratory relief as inherently excessive (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 30, 48). That clause does not confer jurisdiction over anything whatsoever, but it does support a broad remedial mandate once the underlying dispute is within the arbitration agreement (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 30, 48).

D. Conclusion

Meridian has an arguable jurisdiction case, but Atlas is more likely to defeat the core scope objection because English law strongly favours a broad construction of commercial arbitration clauses and the facts point to one integrated dispute rather than disconnected claims (Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40; Arbitration Act 1996, sections 30, 67).

Part III: Arbitrator Impartiality and the Bias Complaint

A. Issue

The next issue is whether one arbitrator's previous professional links with Atlas's counsel required resignation or now justify curial intervention.

B. Rule

The relevant test is objective. The question is whether the fair-minded and informed observer would conclude that there was a real possibility of bias (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48; Porter v Magill [2001] UKHL 67). Prior professional overlap is not automatically disqualifying because arbitration is a specialist field and repeat interaction among counsel and arbitrators is common (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48; Locabail (UK) Ltd v Bayfield Properties Ltd [2000] QB 451).

Halliburton remains central, and the 2025 reforms now add statutory weight by codifying a continuing duty to disclose circumstances that might reasonably give rise to justifiable doubts as to impartiality (Arbitration Act 2025; Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48). That strengthens Meridian's position if disclosure was incomplete, but it does not turn ordinary professional familiarity into automatic disqualification (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48; Locabail (UK) Ltd v Bayfield Properties Ltd [2000] QB 451).

Procedure also matters. If a party learns of the relevant facts during the arbitration, the natural response is to object promptly and, if necessary, seek removal under section 24 of the 1996 Act rather than reserve the point until after an adverse award (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 24, 73). After the award, the complaint is more likely to be framed through section 68 as serious irregularity or, abroad, through the Convention ground concerning tribunal composition, but the party still needs something more substantial than general unease (Arbitration Act 1996, section 68; New York Convention, art V(1)(d)).

C. Application

On the facts given, Meridian's bias argument is possible but presently underdeveloped (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48; Porter v Magill [2001] UKHL 67). "Previous professional links" could mean no more than earlier cases, shared appearances, or ordinary professional familiarity (Locabail (UK) Ltd v Bayfield Properties Ltd [2000] QB 451). In a specialist international arbitration market that will usually be insufficient, especially where the connection is historic, indirect, or fully disclosed (Locabail (UK) Ltd v Bayfield Properties Ltd [2000] QB 451).

Meridian would be in a stronger position if the links were recent, substantial, or undisclosed in circumstances where disclosure was expected (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48; Locabail (UK) Ltd v Bayfield Properties Ltd [2000] QB 451). Repeated appointments involving the same law firm, a continuing advisory relationship, or some financial or strategic dependence might alter the objective appearance (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48; Locabail (UK) Ltd v Bayfield Properties Ltd [2000] QB 451).

The facts as stated do not go that far, but Meridian should investigate the disclosure record closely because a failure to disclose now matters twice over: it sharpens the appearance problem identified in Halliburton (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48) and may also indicate breach of the codified continuing disclosure duty (Arbitration Act 2025).

Atlas's answer is likely to be persuasive unless more emerges (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 24, 73; Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48). It can say that professional familiarity between arbitrators and leading counsel is structurally common, that the law does not impose a rule of automatic resignation whenever overlap exists, and that Meridian either did or should have raised any real objection during the proceedings if the facts were then known (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 24, 73; Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48).

D. Conclusion

Meridian has a live but fact-sensitive impartiality complaint. Without clearer evidence of a serious and inadequately disclosed connection, however, it is more likely to remain supportive background for a fairness challenge than a free-standing winning ground (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48; Porter v Magill [2001] UKHL 67; Arbitration Act 2025).

Part IV: Excluded Evidence, Fair Hearing, and Serious Irregularity

A. Issue

The next issue is whether the exclusion of key evidence denied Meridian a fair opportunity to present its case.

B. Rule

The tribunal has broad powers over procedure and evidence, but those powers are bounded by the general duty to act fairly and give each party a reasonable opportunity to put its case and deal with its opponent's case (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 34). Section 68 is deliberately narrow. It is a long-stop remedy for serious irregularity causing substantial injustice, not a vehicle for relitigating procedural case-management choices simply because the losing party considers them mistaken (Arbitration Act 1996, section 68; Lesotho Highlands Development Authority v Impregilo SpA [2005] UKHL 43).

The same distinction appears in Convention enforcement. A court may refuse enforcement if the resisting party was unable to present its case, but that ground is also interpreted narrowly in pro-enforcement systems (New York Convention, art V(1)(b)). The issue is material procedural unfairness, not whether the tribunal made every procedural choice Meridian preferred (New York Convention, art V(1)(b)).

C. Application

This is potentially Meridian's strongest complaint if the facts support it (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 68; New York Convention, art V(1)(b)). If the excluded evidence went to the heart of jurisdiction, bribery, or the factual basis of lost profits, and if the tribunal excluded it arbitrarily or without giving Meridian any meaningful opportunity to answer the case against it, the resulting unfairness could be serious enough for section 68 relief (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 68).

However, Meridian still faces a high threshold (Arbitration Act 1996, section 68). Tribunals are entitled to manage timetables, exclude late or duplicative material, and impose disciplined evidential boundaries in the interests of efficiency and fairness to both sides (Arbitration Act 1996, section 34). If the evidence was excluded because it was late, procedurally defective, cumulative, or unreliable, Meridian will struggle to convert that into "serious irregularity" (Arbitration Act 1996, section 68; Lesotho Highlands Development Authority v Impregilo SpA [2005] UKHL 43). A robust but defensible case-management decision is not enough merely because Meridian considers the evidence indispensable (Lesotho Highlands Development Authority v Impregilo SpA [2005] UKHL 43).

Atlas therefore has a strong response in principle (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 34, 68). It can emphasise that the parties chose institutional arbitration, accepted arbitral control of procedure, and obtained an award after a hearing in which Meridian participated (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 34). Unless Meridian can show something closer to procedural ambush than ordinary evidential discipline, the English court is unlikely to intervene (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 34, 68).

Meridian may gain extra traction if the fairness complaint can be linked to the jurisdiction complaint (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 68; New York Convention, art V(1)(b)). If the tribunal excluded evidence and then decided issues outside the way Atlas had actually pleaded the case, the cumulative unfairness looks more serious than a simple complaint about admissibility (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 68; New York Convention, art V(1)(b)). But on the facts given that remains a possibility rather than a demonstrated defect (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 33, 68; New York Convention, art V(1)(b)).

D. Conclusion

The evidential exclusion argument is real and should be pursued, but it only becomes a strong fairness ground if Meridian can show both serious procedural unfairness and substantial injustice rather than mere dissatisfaction with a hard procedural ruling (Arbitration Act 1996, section 68; New York Convention, art V(1)(b)).

Part V: Seat Challenge versus Resistance to Enforcement Abroad

A. Issue

The next issue is the practical and legal difference between Meridian's challenge in England and its resistance to enforcement in State Y and other jurisdictions (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V, VI).

B. Rule

Because London is the seat, England is the supervisory forum (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70). The English court can entertain section 67 and section 68 applications and, if persuaded, confirm, vary, remit, set aside, or declare the award ineffective depending on the route and the defect shown (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68).

That is a direct attack on the award (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68). Those applications also sit behind section 70's exhaustion and time-limit requirements, which reinforces that supervisory review is procedurally gated as well as substantively narrow (Arbitration Act 1996, section 70).

Recognition proceedings abroad are different. An enforcing court does not supervise the arbitration as a whole (New York Convention, arts III, V, VI). It decides only whether the award should be recognised and enforced in that jurisdiction within the narrow limits of the New York Convention (New York Convention, arts III, V, VI). A refusal abroad is ordinarily local (New York Convention, arts III, V, VI). By contrast, a successful set-aside at the seat may trigger refusal elsewhere under art V(1)(e), even though that refusal is still technically discretionary rather than automatic (New York Convention, art V(1)(e)).

C. Application

For Meridian, that means England is the main battlefield (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V(1)(e), VI). If Meridian wins under section 67 or section 68, Atlas's position in foreign enforcement proceedings weakens sharply, not only because the award may be remitted or set aside, but also because foreign courts may adjourn or refuse enforcement once the award's status at the seat is compromised (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68; New York Convention, arts V(1)(e), VI).

For Atlas, the distinction is strategically helpful (New York Convention, arts V, VI). Atlas can argue in State Y that Meridian is trying to rerun objections that belong primarily at the seat (Dallah Real Estate and Tourism Holding Co v Ministry of Religious Affairs, Government of Pakistan [2010] UKSC 46; New York Convention, arts V, VI). That is not a complete answer, because Convention grounds remain available abroad even after seat proceedings, but it is a persuasive practical point where the English court has already addressed the same jurisdictional and fairness complaints (Dallah Real Estate and Tourism Holding Co v Ministry of Religious Affairs, Government of Pakistan [2010] UKSC 46; New York Convention, arts V, VI).

The difference also affects the standard of review in substance (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67, as amended by the Arbitration Act 2025; New York Convention, art V). England still treats jurisdiction seriously at the seat, but Meridian should now avoid describing section 67 as though it automatically yields the older style of full rehearing after the tribunal has already ruled on jurisdiction (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67, as amended by the Arbitration Act 2025).

An enforcement court, by contrast, is normally much less willing to let the losing party transform recognition proceedings into a second merits contest (New York Convention, art V). Meridian should not assume that arguments which are arguable in England will travel with equal force at the enforcement stage (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67, as amended by the Arbitration Act 2025; New York Convention, art V).

D. Conclusion

The supervisory review at the seat is the primary attack on the award. Enforcement-stage review abroad is narrower, local, and usually less attractive as a vehicle for broad challenges (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V, VI).

Part VI: Public Policy, Convention Defences, and Multi-State Enforcement

A. Issue

The final major merits issue is whether Meridian can resist recognition in State Y or elsewhere on public-policy or other Convention grounds, and what Atlas should do in response (New York Convention, arts V, VI).

B. Rule

The Convention starts from enforcement. The resisting party bears the burden of proving one of the limited grounds in art V. The important categories here are distinct: art V(1)(b) addresses inability to present the case; art V(1)(c) addresses excess of submission; art V(1)(d) addresses composition or procedure not in accordance with the parties' agreement or the law of the seat; and art V(2)(b) addresses public policy of the enforcing state (New York Convention, arts III, V(1)(b), V(1)(c), V(1)(d), V(2)(b)). Public policy is generally applied narrowly in pro-enforcement jurisdictions because the Convention would otherwise be hollow if every alleged legal or factual error could be re-labelled as public policy (New York Convention, art V(2)(b)).

Article VI adds an important practical mechanism. If proceedings to set aside or suspend the award are pending at the seat, an enforcement court may adjourn the recognition decision and may order the debtor to provide security (New York Convention, art VI). Timing therefore matters as much as doctrine.

C. Application

Meridian's best Convention grounds are art V(1)(b), art V(1)(c), and possibly art V(1)(d), not art V(2)(b) (New York Convention, arts V(1)(b), V(1)(c), V(1)(d), V(2)(b)). The inability-to-present-the-case argument maps onto the evidential exclusion complaint, the excess-of-submission argument maps onto the jurisdiction complaint, and the arbitrator-impartiality/procedure complaint maps most naturally onto art V(1)(d) rather than some free-standing appeal to abstract public policy (New York Convention, arts V(1)(b), V(1)(c), V(1)(d)).

The public-policy argument is weaker unless State Y's own enforcement law is unusually interventionist or the facts conceal something far more troubling than is stated (New York Convention, art V(2)(b)). The relevant question is public policy of the enforcing state, not some abstract transnational objection (New York Convention, art V(2)(b)). An award does not ordinarily offend that public policy simply because it grants lost profits, adopts a view of jurisdiction Meridian dislikes, or rejects certain evidence (New York Convention, art V(2)(b)). Meridian would need something more fundamental, such as enforcement of an award procured by corruption, a clear case of non-arbitrability, or a truly egregious denial of natural justice (New York Convention, art V(2)(b)).

Atlas also has a strong answer on remedies (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 30, 48). Meridian says the tribunal awarded relief on issues not properly submitted, but the award of lost profits and declaratory relief is not objectionable merely because it is substantial (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 30, 48). The contract expressly authorises "all remedies available under the governing law", which makes it harder to characterise the remedial outcome as a jurisdictional overreach once the underlying dispute is within the clause (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 30, 48).

The practical enforcement advice is therefore clear (New York Convention, arts III, VI). Atlas should identify favourable Convention jurisdictions where Meridian has assets, start enforcement quickly, and resist adjournment unless Meridian can show a serious and active challenge at the seat (New York Convention, arts III, VI). If a foreign court is minded to adjourn under art VI, Atlas should press for security so that Meridian cannot use delay as a cheap means of asset protection (New York Convention, arts III, VI).

Meridian, conversely, should avoid a scattershot defence (New York Convention, arts V(1)(b), V(1)(c), V(1)(d), V(2)(b)). Its better strategy is to present a coherent account that the tribunal both exceeded its mandate and denied a fair hearing in ways that matter under identifiable Convention provisions (New York Convention, arts V(1)(b), V(1)(c), V(1)(d)). A loose invocation of "public policy" risks looking like a merits complaint in disguise and is less likely to persuade a pro-enforcement court (New York Convention, arts V(1)(b), V(1)(c), V(1)(d), V(2)(b)).

D. Conclusion

Atlas is more likely than not to achieve enforcement in at least some jurisdictions unless Meridian first gains meaningful traction at the seat (New York Convention, arts V, VI). Meridian's foreign defences are real but narrow, and public policy is the weakest of them on the facts given (New York Convention, art V(2)(b)).

Part VII: Remedies / Liability

Atlas's practical remedies are to defend the award in England, enforce it promptly against reachable assets, and seek security where foreign courts adjourn enforcement pending the English challenge (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 66, 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts III, VI). Enforcement in England under section 66 of the 1996 Act may itself provide useful leverage even though foreign recovery will still depend on local law and Convention procedures (Arbitration Act 1996, section 66).

Meridian's position is narrower (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68). If it succeeds under section 67, the jurisdictional defect may lead to variation or setting aside in whole or in part; if it succeeds under section 68, remittal is often the ordinary response, though more drastic relief remains possible in serious cases (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68). If Meridian fails at the seat, its remaining hope is piecemeal resistance under art V of the Convention, which may still work in a particular state but is unlikely to erase the award globally (New York Convention, art V).

The immediate liability picture is therefore straightforward (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68; New York Convention, arts III, V). Meridian is prima facie liable as award debtor now (Arbitration Act 1996, section 66; New York Convention, art III). Its task is not to reopen the merits generally, but to prove a jurisdictional, procedural, or Convention defect strong enough to displace the award's ordinary enforceability (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68; New York Convention, arts III, V).

Part VIII: Final Conclusion

Atlas is in the stronger overall position (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 1, 67, 68, 70; Arbitration Act 2025). Meridian has some genuinely arguable points, but only a few have real bite (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V, VI).

English arbitration law is structured to protect awards against broad after-the-event attacks, and a current answer must now be framed through the post-2025 regime rather than older shorthand about section 67 rehearings (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67; Arbitration Act 2025). The broad commercial reading of arbitration clauses makes it likely that Atlas's fraud and bribery allegations fall within the clause so long as they arise out of the same contractual relationship and were actually part of the dispute submitted to the tribunal (Fiona Trust & Holding Corpn v Privalov [2007] UKHL 40; Arbitration Act 1996, section 30; Arbitration Act 2025).

Meridian's most serious point is therefore not that fraud is inherently "non-contractual", but that the tribunal may have decided issues that were never properly submitted (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67; New York Convention, art V(1)(c)). If Meridian can identify a genuine excess of reference, section 67 gives it a real route at the seat and art V(1)(c) gives it a corresponding route abroad (Arbitration Act 1996, section 67; New York Convention, art V(1)(c)).

Even then, Meridian must still clear the narrower post-2025 section 67 framework and the section 70 filter, so the argument is dangerous only if the overreach can be shown precisely rather than asserted generally (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 70; Arbitration Act 2025; New York Convention, art V(1)(c)).

The bias complaint is viable only if the professional links with Atlas's counsel were substantial enough, or insufficiently disclosed enough, to create a real possibility of bias in the eyes of the fair-minded and informed observer (Porter v Magill [2001] UKHL 67; Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48). Halliburton remains the leading case on apparent bias and disclosure in this setting (Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd [2020] UKSC 48).

The 2025 codified disclosure duty now makes the disclosure record even more important (Arbitration Act 2025). The fair-hearing complaint about excluded evidence may be stronger, but it still faces the deliberately high threshold of serious irregularity and substantial injustice in England and the narrow due-process ground under the Convention abroad (Arbitration Act 1996, section 68; Lesotho Highlands Development Authority v Impregilo SpA [2005] UKHL 43; New York Convention, art V(1)(b)).

Strategically, the seat challenge matters more than the foreign resistance (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V(1)(e), VI). England is the forum for the primary attack (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70). State Y and other jurisdictions are only forums for collateral resistance (New York Convention, arts V, VI).

Meridian should therefore press its best section 67 and section 68 points first at the seat (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70). Atlas should move quickly in enforcement jurisdictions and oppose adjournment unless Meridian can show a serious live challenge, while seeking security if adjournment is granted (New York Convention, arts III, VI).

If Meridian loses in England, Atlas's path to multi-state enforcement becomes materially easier (New York Convention, arts III, V, VI). If Meridian wins in England, Atlas's foreign enforcement prospects weaken sharply, and other courts may adjourn or refuse enforcement in light of the award's compromised status at the seat (New York Convention, arts V(1)(e), VI; Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70).

The bottom-line advice is therefore as follows. Atlas should defend the award hard at the seat, move quickly where Meridian has assets, and seek security if foreign courts adjourn under art VI (New York Convention, art VI).

Meridian should focus on a disciplined supervisory-court challenge supported by concrete facts on submission, disclosure, and excluded evidence, rather than hope that an abstract appeal to public policy will rescue it abroad (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V(1)(b), V(1)(c), V(1)(d), V(2)(b)). On the facts given, Atlas is more likely than not to preserve and enforce the substantial award, though Meridian retains a serious but limited chance of narrowing or remitting it through the supervisory jurisdiction of the English court (Arbitration Act 1996, sections 67, 68, 70; New York Convention, arts V, VI).
