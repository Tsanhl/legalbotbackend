Part I: Introduction

The daughters should challenge the later will primarily through knowledge and approval, with testamentary capacity as a second strong route and undue influence as a harder alternative. The carer can still succeed if she proves that Margaret gave independent instructions, understood the effect of disinheriting her daughters, and executed the will after a proper private explanation (Wills Act 1837, s 9). The central forensic question is therefore not whether the later will looks morally unattractive, but whether it can be shown to represent Margaret's legally valid and genuinely informed testamentary choice.

That distinction matters because probate disputes often involve overlapping doctrines that perform different legal jobs. Capacity asks whether Margaret could make a will at all. Knowledge and approval asks whether the document truly reflected an informed act of volition. Undue influence asks whether the apparent choice was overborne by coercion. The facts given engage all three, but not with equal force.

Part II: Testamentary Capacity

A. Issue

The first issue is whether Margaret had testamentary capacity when she executed the later will.

B. Rule

Capacity is assessed under the orthodox common-law test. The testator must understand the nature of making a will, the extent of the property, and the claims to which she ought to give effect, while remaining free from a disorder that poisons the affections or distorts judgment (Banks v Goodfellow (1870) LR 5 QB 549). Old age, illness, dependency, and grief do not automatically defeat capacity, but they do require closer scrutiny where the will marks a dramatic departure from an earlier settled scheme (Key v Key [2010] EWHC 408 (Ch)).

The practical emphasis is functional rather than diagnostic. The court is not asking whether Margaret was generally frail or vulnerable. It is asking whether, at the time of execution, she could understand what the new will did and could weigh the competing claims of the daughters and the carer with a sufficiently sound mind (Banks v Goodfellow (1870) LR 5 QB 549). That is why apparently simple facts such as medication, confusion, fatigue, and ability to hold a coherent private discussion with the solicitor become legally decisive.

C. Application

The daughters have a serious point because the later will follows a serious illness and almost wholly displaces the earlier equal division between them. That sort of abrupt change does not prove incapacity, but it does make the court look carefully at cognition, medication, fatigue, and Margaret's ability to weigh the daughters' claims against the carer's position (Banks v Goodfellow (1870) LR 5 QB 549; Key v Key [2010] EWHC 408 (Ch)). The carer will answer that illness alone is insufficient and that the decisive evidence will be the solicitor's attendance note, any medical support, and whether Margaret was able to explain why she preferred the carer despite the obvious competing claims of her children.

The practical evidential questions are concrete. Did Margaret understand that she was making a new will? Did she understand that the daughters would be substantially disinherited? Did she appreciate the broad extent of her estate? Could she articulate a rational reason for preferring the carer? If the solicitor's file answers those questions clearly, the carer gains substantial forensic strength. If the file is thin, generic, or obviously beneficiary-mediated, capacity becomes a live vulnerability rather than a speculative allegation.

The daughters will also rely on the broader precautionary expectations that surround will-making by seriously ill testators. While failure to follow the so-called golden rule does not itself invalidate a will, the absence of careful medical or professional checking can make a contested will much harder to defend when illness and a radical change of testamentary disposition coincide (Key v Key [2010] EWHC 408 (Ch)). If Margaret was not seen properly alone, or if no meaningful attempt was made to assess her understanding despite obvious vulnerability, the daughters' case on capacity becomes materially stronger.

D. Conclusion

Capacity is genuinely contestable. The daughters have a respectable challenge, but its strength will turn on the quality of the contemporaneous medical and solicitor evidence.

Part III: Knowledge and Approval

A. Issue

The next issue is whether the later will truly reflected Margaret's informed wishes once the suspicious circumstances surrounding its preparation are taken into account.

B. Rule

Where a will is duly executed by a capable testator, knowledge and approval is often inferred. That inference weakens where suspicious circumstances surround preparation or execution, especially if a principal beneficiary arranged the process or played a significant part in obtaining instructions (Barry v Butlin (1838) 2 Moo PCC 480). Modern authority explains that suspicious circumstances are not a separate doctrine of invalidity; rather, they intensify scrutiny of whether the propounder has proved a real act of informed volition (Gill v Woodall [2010] EWCA Civ 1430).

The burden is therefore practical and evidential. Once serious suspicion is raised, the propounder of the will must remove it with affirmative evidence showing that the testator actually understood and approved the contents of the instrument (Barry v Butlin (1838) 2 Moo PCC 480; Gill v Woodall [2010] EWCA Civ 1430). The more unusual the beneficiary's role in the preparation process, the more convincing that affirmative evidence must be.

C. Application

This is the daughters' strongest field. The carer arranged the solicitor's visit, remained present for much of the discussion, and emerged as the dominant beneficiary under a will that radically departs from the earlier equal division. Those facts create obvious suspicious circumstances and place a real evidential burden on the carer to show that Margaret gave her own instructions, understood the draft, and approved the final terms after the carer withdrew from the decisive discussion (Barry v Butlin (1838) 2 Moo PCC 480; Gill v Woodall [2010] EWCA Civ 1430).

If the solicitor saw Margaret alone, tested her reasons, read over the will carefully, and recorded a coherent explanation for preferring the carer, the suspicion may be dispelled. If, however, the solicitor relied heavily on the carer for the history, allowed the carer to remain throughout, or failed to record Margaret's reasons for such a radical change, the later will is in real difficulty. The daughters do not need to prove bullying in order to win on this route. They need only show enough suspicion that the carer cannot convincingly prove informed approval.

The daughters can also argue that the later will's structure itself sharpens the suspicion. A modest legacy to the carer might have been easier to defend as gratitude. Near-total displacement of the daughters requires a clearer explanation because it amounts to a radical reordering of Margaret's natural bounty. The stronger the departure from the earlier family scheme, the stronger the court's expectation that the solicitor's file will contain a persuasive record of Margaret's own reasoning (Gill v Woodall [2010] EWCA Civ 1430).

D. Conclusion

Knowledge and approval is the most dangerous issue for the carer because the surrounding circumstances are sufficiently unusual to demand strong affirmative proof.

Part IV: Undue Influence and the Evidential Theme of Suspicion

A. Issue

A further issue is whether the daughters can prove probate undue influence, and how far the same suspicious facts strengthen their overall challenge even if coercion cannot be shown directly.

B. Rule

Probate undue influence is narrower than the equitable doctrine governing lifetime transactions. The challenger must prove coercion that overbore the testator's free will; mere persuasion, gratitude, dependency, or opportunity is insufficient (Edwards v Edwards [2007] EWHC 1119 (Ch)). That is why many probate disputes rely more heavily on knowledge and approval than on undue influence. Suspicious circumstances may reinforce the plausibility of pressure, but they do not by themselves establish coercion (Edwards v Edwards [2007] EWHC 1119 (Ch)).

C. Application

The daughters have enough to plead undue influence responsibly. Serious illness, residential dependency, the carer's role in arranging the solicitor's visit, the carer's presence during much of the discussion, and the dramatic financial benefit all support a narrative of vulnerability and possible domination. Yet the facts as given still fall short of direct proof that Margaret's will was overborne. There is no express evidence of threats, intimidation, isolation from the daughters, or pressure to sign immediately.

That does not make the allegation pointless. In practice, the same facts strengthen the daughters' broader probate case because they reinforce the atmosphere of suspicion and invite the court to examine the solicitor's file, witness evidence, and any medical records with particular care. If later disclosure shows that the carer answered questions for Margaret, blocked private discussion, or pressed for speed, undue influence could become materially stronger. On the present facts, however, it remains the daughters' hardest route and should be pleaded as a serious but secondary alternative.

The carer will try to turn that point around by saying that dependency on a live-in carer is unsurprising after serious illness and that generous testamentary provision for such a person is not inherently suspicious. That is a real counterargument. English law does not require equal treatment of children, and it does not invalidate a will merely because the court or the family consider it unfair. Even so, the daughters still gain from the cumulative evidential picture because the issue is not simply fairness but whether the process was sufficiently independent and voluntary (Edwards v Edwards [2007] EWHC 1119 (Ch)).

D. Conclusion

Undue influence is arguable, but it is currently weaker than the daughters' knowledge-and-approval case because coercion, not mere opportunity, must be shown.

Part V: Remedies / Liability

If the daughters defeat the later will for lack of capacity, lack of knowledge and approval, or undue influence, the later instrument should not be admitted to probate. The ordinary result is that the earlier valid will governs, because the ineffective later instrument cannot safely revoke it as a valid testamentary act (Wills Act 1837, s 9). On these facts that means the estate would most likely revert to the earlier equal-division will, not pass to the carer and not ordinarily fall into intestacy.

The carer, by contrast, will seek probate in solemn form and rely on execution formalities, the solicitor's note, and any medical support to show that the later will was validly made and should be pronounced for. The litigation therefore turns less on abstract doctrine than on whether the evidential record is strong enough to dispel the obvious suspicions surrounding the new will. In practical terms, disclosure of the solicitor's attendance notes, draft history, and any medical communications is likely to be the decisive battlefield.

The daughters should also be clear about the practical binary that follows from success or failure. If the later will stands, the carer takes almost everything under it. If the later will fails, the earlier will is the obvious destination unless some separate challenge is mounted against that earlier instrument. Nothing in the problem suggests such a further attack. The real remedies analysis is therefore not a complicated menu of possible outcomes, but a contest between probate of the later will and probate of the earlier equal-division will.

That practical focus should shape how the parties prepare the case. The daughters should concentrate on obtaining the solicitor's attendance note, any draft chronology, and evidence about whether Margaret was seen alone. The carer should concentrate on demonstrating independence of instruction and coherent explanation for the change in testamentary scheme. In probate litigation of this kind, the most powerful evidence is often not a dramatic witness allegation but the quality, or weakness, of the contemporaneous file.

Part VI: Final Conclusion

The daughters have a strong overall challenge. Their best route is knowledge and approval, because the combination of serious illness, beneficiary involvement, presence during much of the discussion, and a radical departure from the earlier will creates substantial suspicious circumstances that the carer must dispel (Barry v Butlin (1838) 2 Moo PCC 480; Gill v Woodall [2010] EWCA Civ 1430). Testamentary capacity is also a real and potentially strong issue if the solicitor's file or medical evidence is weak (Banks v Goodfellow (1870) LR 5 QB 549; Key v Key [2010] EWHC 408 (Ch)).

Undue influence should be pleaded, but realistically as the hardest allegation because coercion must be shown rather than dependency or persuasion (Edwards v Edwards [2007] EWHC 1119 (Ch)). If the daughters succeed on any of those grounds, the earlier will dividing the estate equally between them is likely to control the estate. If the solicitor's evidence is careful, independent, and well documented, the carer may still defend the later will.

The practical ranking of arguments is therefore clear. First, attack knowledge and approval. Secondly, press capacity if the contemporaneous evidence is weak. Thirdly, maintain undue influence as a serious alternative in case disclosure reveals stronger evidence of domination. On the facts given, however, the daughters begin in the stronger forensic position because the carer bears the heavier burden of explaining a highly unusual testamentary process and outcome.

That is why the daughters' best litigation strategy is cumulative rather than single-track. Each doctrine reinforces the others, and together they force the carer to justify both the process and the result. On the present facts, that combined burden is substantial and difficult to discharge.
